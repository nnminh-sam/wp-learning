Back-of-envelope estimation

back-of-envelope-estimation · v1 · use · 14 min read · 4 diagrams

Back-of-envelope estimation is quick, rough arithmetic that turns "lots of users" into a few numbers: requests per second, gigabytes stored, megabytes per second sent. You round hard, write your assumptions down, and aim to be right within a factor of 2 to 10, not to the last digit S0020S0021S0025. It matters because those numbers decide the design. One server or many? Cache or no cache? Database or object storage? S0021S0015

★ Your questions, answered#1 min

1. What is the "equation" for estimating a feature? It is a short chain of multiplications and one division. Start from users, turn them into actions, then into requests per second, bytes stored and bytes sent S0025S0020S0021. The one trick: a day has 86,400 seconds, so call it 10^5 seconds S0021.

2. Which numbers decide the scale? There are two kinds of numbers, and you need both.

KindThe numbersWhere they come from
Inputs (you assume them)DAU, actions per user, read:write ratio, item size, retention, copies, peak factorYou state them, the interviewer can change them S0021S0025
Outputs (you compute them)Peak QPS (reads and writes), total storage, peak bandwidth, size of the hot dataThe chain above S0020S0021
Yardsticks (you memorise them)What one app server, database or Redis can do; latency of memory vs disk vs networkReference tables below S0015S0019S0023

The decision always comes from putting an output next to a yardstick S0015. "600 QPS" means nothing alone. "600 QPS against a database that handles tens of thousands" means "one database is enough" S0015.

3. What does "back-of-envelope" mean? Literally, a sum small enough to scribble on the back of an envelope. Jeff Dean, a Google Senior Fellow, defined it as "estimates you create using a combination of thought experiments and common performance numbers to get a good feel for which designs will meet your requirements" S0020. He made it popular in his talks on building large systems at Google S0024S0019. It is not precise maths. It is a quick, approximate check that a design is feasible S0022.

Core ideas#8 min

What back-of-envelope meanscore concept#

In shortQuick, rounded arithmetic from stated assumptions and common performance numbers (Jeff Dean's definition) that aims for the right order of magnitude, to judge which designs can meet the requirements.

The goal is the right order of magnitude: is it about 10, 1,000 or 1,000,000? Whether something is ten thousand or a billion changes the architecture. Whether it is 1.1 million or 1.3 million usually does not S0025.

Interviewers watch the process more than the result: make assumptions, say them out loud, keep moving S0020S0025.

Example A one-line estimate

"How long does it take to read 1 GB from an SSD?" SSDs read about 1 GB/s sequentially, so about 1 second. From an old spinning disk at about 30 MB/s, it takes about 30 seconds S0001.

Round, state, labelconcept#

In shortRound to powers of 10 while calculating (1 day ≈ 10^5 s, 1M/day ≈ 10/s), write every assumption down, and put a unit on every number.

Three habits make the arithmetic easy and safe S0020S0021:

  • Round while you calculate, not at the end. "99,987 / 9.1" becomes "100,000 / 10" S0020. 1,825 TB becomes 2 PB straight away S0021.
  • Write the assumptions down so you and the interviewer can point at them later S0020S0025.
  • Label every unit. A bare "5" could be 5 KB or 5 MB S0020.

Work in powers of 10. Multiply the leading digits, then add the exponents: 2 x 10^6 users x 10 actions = 2 x 10^7 actions S0021.

Handy round numberValueWhy it helps
Seconds in a day86,400 ≈ 10^5Requests/day ÷ 10^5 = QPS S0021S0025
Seconds in a year≈ 31.5 million ≈ 3 x 10^7Yearly totals S0025
1 million per day≈ 10 per second10^6 ÷ 10^5 = 10 (really ~12) S0021
1 million x 1 KB= 1 GB10^6 x 10^3 bytes = 10^9 bytes S0020
Days in 5 years365 x 5 ≈ 1,800 ≈ 2,000Retention totals S0021
Tip The 10^5 shortcut

Dividing by 10^5 instead of 86,400 makes your QPS about 15% too low. That is far inside the "within 2x" target. Say "about" and move on S0021S0020.

QPS from daily active userscore concept#

In shortAverage QPS = DAU x requests per user per day ÷ 10^5; peak QPS = average x a stated peak factor (2x-10x); compute reads and writes separately to see the read:write ratio.

QPS means queries (requests) per second. Average QPS = DAU x requests per user per day ÷ 10^5 S0025S0020. If you only have monthly users, first multiply by the share who come each day S0020.

Then apply a peak factor. Traffic is not flat across the day, so systems must handle bursts S0025. Sources use 2x S0020, 3x S0021 or anywhere from 3x to 10x S0025. Pick one, say it, and move on.

Finally, split reads from writes. Most apps read far more than they write. A photo app with 100 reads per write ends up with 300 writes per second but 30,000 reads per second at peak S0021. That ratio tells you where to spend design effort: here, on the read path S0021.

Example 10M users, 20 requests each

10M x 20 = 200M requests/day. 200M ÷ 10^5 = 2,000 QPS (exact: ~2,300). Peak at 5x ≈ 10,000-11,500 QPS S0025.

Storage estimatecore concept#

In shortStorage = item size x new items per day x days retained x copies; estimate item size from its fields, and do not apply the peak factor to storage.

Storage = size of one item x new items per day x days kept x copies S0021S0025.

Estimate the item size from its fields. A post-like record might be an 8-byte user ID, an 8-byte timestamp, 200 bytes of text and 100 bytes of metadata, so about 300 bytes S0025. Media is much bigger: an image is 100 KB to 1 MB, a minute of video 5-10 MB S0025.

Two rules are easy to forget. First, multiply by the number of copies (replicas, backups): 1 TB of raw data can mean 3 TB or more S0025S0021. Second, no peak factor for storage: a day of uploads is the same size whether it arrives smoothly or in bursts S0021.

Example Photo app storage S0021

10M photos/day x 500 KB = 5 TB/day. x 365 ≈ 2 PB/year. x 5 years = 10 PB. x 3 copies = 30 PB.

Bandwidth estimateconcept#

In shortBandwidth = peak QPS x payload per request, in and out separately; network links are quoted in bits, so multiply bytes per second by 8.

Bandwidth = QPS x bytes per request. Do it separately for incoming (writes) and outgoing (reads) data S0021S0025. Use peak QPS, because the network must cope with the peak S0021.

Watch bits vs bytes. One byte is 8 bits S0020. Storage is quoted in bytes (MB, GB). Network links are quoted in bits (Mbps, Gbps). So 500 MB/s is about 4 Gbps S0025. A 1 Gbps link moves roughly 100 MB/s S0001.

Example Photo app bandwidth S0021

In: 100 writes/s x 500 KB = 50 MB/s. Out: 10,000 reads/s x 500 KB = 5 GB/s. At 3x peak: 150 MB/s in, 15 GB/s out. The 100x gap between in and out equals the read:write ratio, a good sign the sums are consistent.

Latency orders of magnitudecore concept#

In shortRAM is ~100 ns, an SSD random read ~150 µs, a same-datacenter round trip ~0.5 ms, an HDD seek ~10 ms and a cross-region round trip 50-150 ms; know the ranking and the gaps, not the digits.

You do not need every row of the famous latency table. You need the ranking and the rough gaps between rows S0022S0020. Each line below is roughly 10x to 100x slower than the one above it.

OperationRough timeThink of it as
Read main memory (RAM)100 nsnanoseconds S0019
Read 4 KB at random from SSD150 µs~1,000x RAM S0019S0001
Round trip inside one datacenter500 µs (sub-1 ms)under a millisecond S0019S0023
Read 1 MB sequentially from SSD1 msS0019
Spinning-disk (HDD) seek10 ms~20x a datacenter round trip S0019
Packet California → Netherlands → California150 mscross-region: 50-150 ms S0019S0023

Lessons: memory is fast, disk is slow, avoid disk seeks, and calls between regions are slow S0020. You get about 2,000 round trips per second inside a datacenter, but only 6-7 around the world S0001.

Example Why a cache helps

A Redis read is a network hop plus a RAM lookup, about 1 ms S0015. A trip to another region is 50-150 ms S0023. That is 50-150x slower. This gap is why you cache near the user or put a CDN in front S0015.

Powers of two and data unitsconcept#

In shortEvery 10 powers of two is about 1,000: 2^10 ≈ KB, 2^20 ≈ MB, 2^30 ≈ GB, 2^40 ≈ TB, 2^50 ≈ PB; 2^32 ≈ 4.3 billion.

Every 10 powers of two is about 1,000 S0001S0020:

PowerApprox.Unit
2^101 thousand (10^3)1 KB
2^201 million (10^6)1 MB
2^301 billion (10^9)1 GB
2^401 trillion (10^12)1 TB
2^501 quadrillion (10^15)1 PB

Two extras from the same table: 2^16 = 65,536 (64 KB) and 2^32 ≈ 4.3 billion (4 GB) S0001. One ASCII character is one byte S0020. For estimates, treat 1 KB as 1,000 bytes; storage and bandwidth are usually counted in base 10 anyway S0025S0022.

Example A JS developer's version

A 2^32 counter (an unsigned 32-bit ID) runs out at about 4 billion S0001. 300M new tweets a day S0020 would use it up in about 14 days. That is why such systems use 64-bit IDs.

Availability in ninesconcept#

In shortEach extra nine cuts downtime 10x (99.9% ≈ 8.8 h/year, 99.99% ≈ 53 min/year); components in sequence multiply availability down, components in parallel raise it.

Availability is the share of time a service is up, counted in "nines": 99.99% is "four nines" S0001. Big cloud providers set their SLAs at 99.9% or higher S0020.

AvailabilityDowntime per yearDowntime per day
99%3.65 days14.4 min S0020
99.9% (three nines)~8 h 46 min~1 min 26 s S0001
99.99% (four nines)~52.6 min~8.6 s S0001S0020
99.999% (five nines)~5.3 min~0.9 s S0020

Shortcut: every extra nine divides the downtime by 10 S0020. Parts that run in sequence multiply: two 99.9% parts in a chain give about 99.8% S0001. Parts in parallel (either one can serve) give 1 - (0.001 x 0.001) = 99.9999% S0001.

Example Your Next.js app

Browser → NestJS API (99.9%) → Postgres (99.9%) are in sequence, so the whole request path is about 99.8% available. Two API instances behind a load balancer are in parallel, which is far better than one S0001.

What one component can handleconcept#

In shortModern rough limits: a SQL database up to ~50K transactions/s and a few TB per instance, Redis 100K+ ops/s, an app server 100K+ connections; older tables quote far lower numbers.

These are the yardsticks for the "compare and decide" step. Modern hardware is much stronger than many candidates assume S0015S0023.

ComponentRough capacity (modern)Scale out when…
App server100K+ concurrent connections; 64-512 GB RAMCPU > 70%, or latency over the SLA S0015
SQL database (Postgres/MySQL)up to ~50K transactions/s; a few TB comfortably on one instancewrites > 10K/s, or data in the tens of TB S0015
Cache (Redis)100K+ operations/s; ~1 ms; up to ~1 TB of memoryhit rate < 80% or memory > 80% S0015
One big server512 GiB RAM, 128 vCPUs; 25 Gbps networkS0023

A worked server count: "50K requests per second, each server handles maybe 5K, so about 10 servers plus some headroom" S0015.

Watch out Older tables give smaller numbers

One course still lists about 1,000 QPS for MySQL and 10,000 for a key-value store S0022. With numbers like those you will shard and cache far earlier than you need to S0015S0023. See "Where sources disagree".

From estimate to decisioncore concept#

In shortPut each estimate next to a component's limit: under it, keep one box; reads far above writes point to caching and replicas; tens of TB or 10K+ writes/s point to splitting data; petabytes of files point to object storage.

An estimate is only useful when it changes the design S0004S0021. Put each output next to a yardstick and read off the decision:

If the estimate says…It points to…
Peak QPS well under one DB/server's limitKeep one box (plus a spare for availability) S0015
Reads ≫ writes (e.g. 30,000 vs 300 per second)Read path first: a cache, read replicas S0021
Writes > ~10K/s, or data in tens of TBSplit the data across machines (sharding, a later topic) S0015
Petabytes of filesObject storage for the bytes; the DB keeps only small records S0021
Hot data fits in memory (GBs, not TBs)One cache instance can hold it S0021S0015
Gigabytes per second going outA CDN, so bytes are served near users S0021

Interviewers change one input on purpose to see if you understand the chain S0021. If the photo app's uploads go up 5x, every number goes up 5x, but the decisions stay the same S0021.

When to estimate, and when not toconcept#

In shortEstimate the numbers that can change a decision, when they are needed; skip or shorten estimates that only conclude 'it's a lot'. Sources disagree on doing it upfront.

Hello Interview says to skip the big upfront estimate. Do the maths only when it will change a decision, such as whether one Top-K heap fits on one machine S0004S0015. Their complaint: candidates compute DAU, QPS and storage, then say "ok, so it's a lot" and design the same thing anyway S0004.

ByteByteGo puts the estimate into step 2 of its framework, to check that the first design fits the scale, but also says to ask the interviewer whether it is needed S0014. It lists QPS, peak QPS, storage, cache and server count as commonly asked S0020.

Interview angle What a strong answer sounds like

"Let me estimate just the numbers that matter here: peak read QPS and 5-year storage. If reads stay under ~10K/s and the data under a few TB, one Postgres with a replica is enough, and I'll say so." Then actually make the decision S0004S0015S0021.

Worked example 1: a Twitter-like service (classic)#1 min

The numbers are for practice only, not real Twitter data S0020. Assumptions: 300M monthly active users, 50% use it daily, 2 tweets per user per day, 10% of tweets carry media, data kept 5 years S0020.

Example So what does this decide?
  • 55 PB of media is far too big for any database. The files go to object storage, and the database keeps only small records that point to them S0021S0023.
  • ~110 TB of tweet rows is well past the "few TB" one Postgres instance handles comfortably, so the tweet table must be spread over several machines S0015.
  • ~7,000 writes/s at peak is just under the ~10K/s write trigger S0015. Writes alone are not the problem; storage size is.
  • Reading timelines will be many times the write load (we did not estimate it here), which points to caching. Later nodes cover how.

Worked example 2: product reviews in a shopping app#1 min

This one is closer to what you build: a Next.js product page that calls a NestJS API, which reads reviews from Postgres. The numbers below are my own assumptions, chosen to be realistic for a mid-size app. The method is the one from S0021 and S0025.

AssumptionValue
DAU2 million
Product pages viewed per user per day10 (each page loads one page of reviews)
Users who write a review on a given day1%
One review on disk~1 KB (3 IDs x 8 B + rating + ~500 B text + timestamps ≈ 550 B, rounded up for indexes)
Reviews per API response20, so ~20 KB

Kept5 years, 3 copies (primary + 2 replicas)
Example So what does this decide?
  • One Postgres is plenty. 600 reads/s is far under what one database can do S0015. Even the old, cautious figure of ~1,000 QPS for MySQL covers it S0022.
  • No sharding. 40 GB (120 GB with replicas) is far below "a few TB" per instance S0015. The whole table would even fit in one server's RAM S0023.
  • A cache is optional, not forced. Add one for speed if you like, but the numbers do not require it S0015.
  • Run two NestJS instances anyway, for availability, not for capacity: two in parallel beat one S0001.
  • What would change the answer? At 20M DAU and a 10x sale peak, reads reach 20,000/s. Now a cache and read replicas are worth discussing S0021S0015. The method is the same; only one input changed.

Common misconceptions#1 min

Common mix-up "I need the exact answer."

You need the right order of magnitude. Rounding "99,987 / 9.1" to "100,000 / 10" is expected S0020. Carrying three significant figures through guesses gives a false sense of precision S0021.

Common mix-up "Average QPS is what I design for."

Systems fall over at the peak. Always multiply by a peak factor (2x-10x) and say which one you chose S0025S0020.

Common mix-up "MB/s and Mbps are the same."

One byte is 8 bits S0020. 500 MB/s is about 4 Gbps S0025. Mixing them up gives an 8x error.

Common mix-up "Storage is size x items. Done."

Also multiply by retention and copies: 10 PB of photos becomes 30 PB with three copies S0021S0025. But do not apply the peak factor to storage S0021.

Common mix-up "A database tops out around 1,000 QPS, so shard early."

That is an old number. A well-tuned database handles tens of thousands of queries per second, and Redis hundreds of thousands S0015S0023. Sharding at 500 GB adds complexity for no reason S0015.

Common mix-up "Estimation is the goal."

The estimate is a tool to make a decision. "Ok, so it's a lot" earns nothing S0004. "So the bytes go to object storage" does S0021.

Where sources disagree or are unclear#1 min

  • Estimate upfront or only when needed? Hello Interview says skip the upfront estimate and calculate only when it changes a decision S0004S0015. ByteByteGo puts it in step 2 to check the design fits the scale, while also saying to ask first S0014S0020. The System Design Handbook says interviewers often ask for it early, and that it becomes "non-negotiable" from mid-level up S0025. Safe path: ask, then estimate only the numbers that drive a decision.
  • How strong is one database? About 1,000 QPS for MySQL in one course S0022, against up to ~50K transactions/s in another S0015. The second is newer and explicitly warns about outdated numbers S0023. Use the modern figure, but know the old one exists.
  • Latency inside a datacenter. 500 µs in the classic table S0019S0001; sub-1 ms within one zone and 1-2 ms across zones S0023; "1-10 ms" for network calls in another source S0015. All agree it is about 1,000x RAM and far below cross-region.
  • Small differences in the latency table. Mutex lock/unlock is 100 ns in one copy S0020 and 25 ns in another S0019. Reading 1 MB from disk is 30 ms S0020S0001 or 20 ms S0019. One course gives very different values, such as 2 ms for 1 MB from disk S0022. Use the ranking, not the digits.
  • Peak factor. 2x S0020, 3x S0021, 3-10x S0025. There is no single right value.
